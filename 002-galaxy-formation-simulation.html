Overview: The Cosmic Web in a Box

How did the majestic galaxies we see today, including our own Milky Way, arise from a nearly uniform, hot, dense early universe? This simulation provides a window into that process. It models a representative cube of the universe, filled with dark matter and gas, and lets it evolve over billions of years. You are watching gravitational instability at work: regions that were initially just infinitesimally denser than average pull in more and more material. Over cosmic time, this relentless pull sculpts the matter into a vast, intricate network of filaments, clusters, and voids known as the "Cosmic Web." Galaxies form at the intersections of these filaments, like cities at the junctions of a national highway system. This simulation uses the standard model of cosmology, called Lambda-CDM (ΛCDM), which includes Dark Energy (Λ) and Cold Dark Matter (CDM), to create a scientifically-grounded picture of cosmic structure formation.

Core Theory: Gravity in an Expanding Universe

Simulating galaxy formation requires combining Newtonian gravity with the expansion of the universe, as described by Einstein's theory of General Relativity.

The Expanding Background

The universe is expanding. We describe this expansion using a dimensionless scale factor, a(t), which is 1 at the present day and was smaller in the past. The evolution of a(t) is governed by the Friedmann equation:

(ȧ/a)² = H₀² [ Ωm/a³ + Ωr/a⁴ + Ωk/a² + ΩΛ ]

Where ȧ is the time derivative of a, H₀ is the Hubble constant, and the Ω terms represent the fractional density of matter, radiation, curvature, and dark energy today. In this simulation, we simplify to a flat universe (Ωk=0) and ignore the radiation-dominated era (Ωr=0), which is a good approximation for the period of galaxy formation.

Equations of Motion in Comoving Coordinates

It's computationally convenient to factor out the expansion. We use "comoving coordinates" (x), which are a particle's physical coordinates (r) divided by the scale factor: r = a(t)x. A particle at rest in the expansion has a constant comoving position. The equation of motion for a particle i in comoving coordinates, under the influence of gravity from other particles, becomes:

ẍ + 2(ȧ/a)ẋ = - (1/a³) ∇xΦpec

This looks complicated, but it has three parts: the particle's comoving acceleration (ẍ), a "Hubble drag" term that slows particles down due to cosmic expansion (2(ȧ/a)ẋ), and the gravitational force on the right. The gravitational potential Φpec is the "peculiar" potential, sourced by the density fluctuations (δρ) away from the uniform average density.

The Particle-Mesh (PM) Gravity Solver

Calculating the force on every particle from every other particle (N²) is too slow for thousands of particles. We use a more efficient Particle-Mesh (PM) algorithm:

  1. Mass Assignment: We create a grid. Each particle's mass is assigned to the nearby grid points. A common scheme is Cloud-in-Cell (CIC), where a particle is treated as a uniform square cloud and its mass is distributed to the four grid cells it overlaps.
  2. Solve for Potential: We now have a density field on the grid, ρ(x,y). This density sources the gravitational potential via the Poisson equation: ∇²Φ = 4πGρ. This equation is difficult to solve directly, but becomes a simple multiplication in Fourier space. We use a Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) on the density grid, solve for the potential in Fourier space, and then use an inverse FFT to get the potential back on our real-space grid.
  3. Calculate Force: The force is the negative gradient of the potential (F = -∇Φ). We can calculate this on the grid using finite differences (e.g., Fx(i,j) = -(Φi+1,j - Φi-1,j) / (2 * grid_spacing)).
  4. Interpolate Force: We now have the gravitational force defined at every grid point. To get the force at a specific particle's position, we interpolate the force from the surrounding grid points back to the particle, using the same scheme as in step 1.

This PM method is much faster (scaling as N log N due to the FFT) and is a workhorse of modern cosmological simulations.

Deep Q&A

1. Why is it called "Cold" Dark Matter (ΛCDM)?

The "Cold" in Cold Dark Matter (CDM) refers to the velocity of the dark matter particles. In this model, dark matter particles were moving very slowly ("non-relativistically") at the time when cosmic structures began to form. This is a crucial detail. If dark matter were "Hot" (i.e., moving near the speed of light, like neutrinos), its rapid motion would smooth out small, initial density fluctuations. This means that in a Hot Dark Matter universe, large structures like superclusters would have to form first and then fragment into smaller things like galaxies. This is called a "top-down" formation scenario. However, our observations of the universe, including the properties of galaxies and the cosmic microwave background, strongly support a "bottom-up" scenario where small clumps of cold dark matter formed first and then merged over time to build progressively larger structures (halos, galaxies, clusters). The simulation you are watching is a direct visualization of this bottom-up process, made possible by the "cold" nature of the dark matter.
