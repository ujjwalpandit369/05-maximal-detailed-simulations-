Overview: Building the Skeletons of Galaxies

Galaxies like our Milky Way are not isolated islands of stars; they are embedded within vast, invisible spheres of dark matter called "halos." These halos are the true building blocks of the universe, acting as gravitational anchors that pull in gas and form the galaxies we see. This simulation shows you how a single dark matter halo is born. We start with a region of the early universe that is slightly denser than its surroundings. You will watch as gravity takes over, pulling these dark matter particles together in a process called "gravitational collapse." The initial collapse is violent, but the system eventually settles into a stable, or "virialized," state. The key part of this simulation is the analysis tools. You can measure the halo's density from its core to its outskirts and compare it to famous theoretical models like the Navarro-Frenk-White (NFW) profile. You can also measure its rotation curve, the key piece of evidence that first pointed to the existence of dark matter. This is a hands-on exploration of the fundamental structures that underpin everything we see in the cosmos.

Core Theory: Spherical Collapse and Virialization

The formation of a dark matter halo can be understood through the elegant model of spherical collapse.

1. The Spherical "Top-Hat" Model

We start by considering an idealized, perfectly spherical region of uniform overdensity δ in an otherwise uniform, expanding universe. Because this region is denser than the background, its self-gravity is stronger than the cosmic average. While the rest of the universe continues to expand forever (in a ΛCDM model), the gravitational pull on this "top-hat" sphere is strong enough to slow its expansion, halt it, and eventually reverse it into a collapse. The moment of maximum expansion is called "turnaround."

2. Collapse and Violent Relaxation

As the sphere collapses under its own gravity, it doesn't just shrink to a point. The particles overshoot the center, and the system undergoes a chaotic phase called "violent relaxation." During this phase, the rapidly changing gravitational potential efficiently redistributes energy among the particles. Individual particles have their energies and angular momenta significantly altered, and the system quickly settles into a stable, long-lived equilibrium state. This process erases much of the "memory" of the initial conditions.

3. Virialization and the Virial Theorem

The final stable state is known as a "virialized" halo. The Virial Theorem relates the time-averaged kinetic energy (<T>) and potential energy (<U>) of a stable, self-gravitating system: 2<T> = -<U>. This means the halo is in a state of statistical equilibrium, where the random motions of the particles (their kinetic energy, which acts like a pressure) are sufficient to support the structure against its own gravity (potential energy). The radius at which this condition is met is called the virial radius, Rvir, and it encloses a mean density that is roughly 200 times the critical density of the universe at that time.

4. The "Universal" Density Profile (NFW)

A remarkable discovery from large-scale N-body simulations in the 1990s was that all dark matter halos, regardless of their mass or formation time, seem to share a similar internal density structure. This is known as the Navarro-Frenk-White (NFW) profile:

ρ(r) = ρ0 / [ (r/rs) * (1 + r/rs)² ]

Where ρ0 is a characteristic density and rs is a scale radius. This profile has a "cuspy" behavior, where the density ρ(r) ∝ 1/r near the center, and falls off as ρ(r) ∝ 1/r³ in the outskirts. The shape of the profile is often described by a single "concentration" parameter, c = Rvir / rs. This simulation allows you to calculate the density profile of the halo you form and fit it to the NFW model to find its concentration.

5. Circular Velocity Curves

The density profile directly determines the circular velocity curve, Vc(r), which is the orbital speed needed to maintain a circular orbit at radius r. It's calculated as Vc(r) = √(GM(<r)/r), where M(<r) is the total mass enclosed within radius r. For an NFW halo, this curve rises from the center, reaches a peak, and then becomes nearly flat in the outer regions. It was exactly this type of flat rotation curve observed in real galaxies that provided the first strong evidence for the existence of dark matter far beyond the visible stars.
