Overview: The Brightest Engines in the Universe

Accretion disks are nature's way of feeding giant objects. When gas and dust fall towards a black hole, a young star, or a neutron star, they rarely fall straight in. Instead, because of conservation of angular momentum, the material flattens into a swirling, pancake-like structure: an accretion disk. This simulation models the physics of such a disk. The key ingredient is viscosity—a kind of friction—which allows gas particles to rub against each other, converting their orbital energy into heat. This process makes the disk glow incredibly brightly, often outshining an entire galaxy, and it allows particles to lose momentum and spiral inwards, eventually feeding the central object. You will control the viscosity and see how it dictates the structure, temperature, and brightness of the disk. This is a laboratory for understanding the physics behind protoplanetary disks where planets are born, and the quasars that are the most luminous objects in the universe.

Core Theory: The Physics of Viscous Accretion

An accretion disk is a complex system where orbital mechanics meets fluid dynamics.

1. Keplerian Orbits

To a first approximation, each particle of gas in the disk wants to follow a simple circular orbit, as described by Kepler's laws. The orbital velocity v at a radius r from a central mass M is given by v = √(GM/r). This means inner parts of the disk rotate much faster than outer parts. This difference in velocity, called "differential rotation," is the ultimate source of the disk's activity.

2. The Angular Momentum Problem

A particle in a circular orbit has a specific angular momentum. To move to a smaller orbit (i.e., to accrete), it must lose angular momentum. But angular momentum is a conserved quantity. It can't just disappear. It must be transported away. This is the central problem of accretion theory. The solution is viscosity.

3. Viscosity and Angular Momentum Transport

Viscosity acts as a frictional force between adjacent, differentially rotating layers of gas. The fast-moving inner gas tries to speed up the slower-moving outer gas, and the outer gas tries to slow down the inner gas. This has two effects:

  • The inner gas particle loses angular momentum and spirals inwards.
  • The outer gas particle gains that angular momentum and spirals outwards.

For accretion to happen, the vast majority of the disk's mass slowly spirals inward, while a tiny amount of mass at the outer edge carries away almost all the angular momentum. The true source of this viscosity in real disks is thought to be the Magneto-Rotational Instability (MRI), a complex process involving magnetic fields. In this simulation, we use the famous Shakura-Sunyaev α-disk model, which parameterizes this unknown viscosity with a single knob, α.

4. Energy Dissipation and Temperature Profile

The "friction" from viscosity generates heat, converting the gravitational potential energy of the accreting gas into thermal energy. This energy is then radiated away from the disk's surface. The rate of energy dissipation can be calculated, and for a standard thin disk, it leads to a characteristic temperature profile:

T(r) ∝ (GMṀ/r³)1/4 ∝ r-3/4

Where Ṁ (M-dot) is the mass accretion rate. This means the inner parts of the disk are dramatically hotter than the outer parts. This temperature profile is a key prediction and can be observed in real systems. You can verify this relationship in the "Disk Profiles" chart.
