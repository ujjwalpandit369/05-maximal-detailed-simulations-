The Deep Dive: Wick Rotation and the Nature of Time

Beginner's Guide: How Space Becomes Time

Think about how you move. You can go forward, backward, left, right, up, or down. These are the three dimensions of space. You can move any way you like in them and return to your starting point. Now think about time. You can only move forward. You can't stop, go backward, or move "sideways" in time. This is the fundamental difference between space and time.

In physics, this difference is captured by a simple minus sign. When we calculate the "distance" between two events in spacetime, we add the squares of the spatial distances but *subtract* the square of the time separation. This is called the Lorentzian metric. A universe without that minus sign, where time is treated just like another spatial dimension, is called a Euclidean universe.

The Hartle-Hawking "no-boundary" idea proposes that our universe began in a Euclidean state. There was no time, only four dimensions of space. In this state, there was no "before" or "after," no cause and effect. It was a static, timeless geometry. This simulation lets you visualize this state as a sphere. Then, through a process called a "Wick Rotation" (controlled by the main slider), one of these spatial dimensions transformed into time. This introduced the crucial minus sign into the equations of spacetime. As you move the slider, you see the sphere morph into a hyperboloid (a Pringle-like shape), which represents our familiar expanding universe. The vertical direction on the hyperboloid is time, and the horizontal circles are slices of space at a particular moment. The emergence of time is the emergence of this unique direction, and with it, the entire history of the cosmos.

Core Theory: The Metric Tensor and Signature Change

The geometry of spacetime is defined by the metric tensor, gμν. This tensor tells us how to compute the infinitesimal distance, ds, between two nearby points. In the flat spacetime of special relativity (Minkowski space), this is given by:
ds² = -c²dt² + dx² + dy² + dz²
The set of signs (-,+,+,+) is called the *signature* of the metric. The lone minus sign for the time component is what makes time unique. It defines the causal structure of the universe, creating a "light cone" of past and future events. All of cause and effect is contained in this minus sign.

A Euclidean 4-space, by contrast, has the metric:
ds² = dτ² + dx² + dy² + dz²
Here, the signature is (+,+,+,+). The coordinate τ is just another spatial dimension, indistinguishable from the others. There are no light cones, and thus no concept of causality.

The transition between these two is achieved by a Wick rotation. This is a mathematical transformation where we substitute the time coordinate t with an imaginary counterpart, t → iτ. Let's see what this does to the metric:
ds² = -c²(iτ)² + dx² + ... = -c²(i²)(dτ)² + dx² + ... = -c²(-1)(dτ)² + dx² + ... = +c²dτ² + dx² + ...
The minus sign has vanished! The metric has become Euclidean. The no-boundary proposal posits that the universe began in a state described by a Euclidean metric and then underwent a process equivalent to a Wick rotation back to a Lorentzian metric, which we now experience.

This simulation visualizes this transition through a parameter α (controlled by the slider). We can imagine a toy metric where the time component's sign is controlled by α:
ds² = (2α - 1)c²dt² + dx² + dy² + dz²

  • When α = 0, ds² = -c²dt² + ..., which is our familiar Lorentzian spacetime.
  • When α = 1, ds² = +c²dt² + ..., which is a Euclidean spacetime.
  • When α = 0.5, the time component vanishes entirely. This is a singular moment where the dimension of spacetime itself changes.
The 3D surface on the canvas is an *embedding diagram* that attempts to show the curvature of this (t, x, y) space. The sphere represents the compact, boundary-less Euclidean geometry. The hyperboloid represents the expanding Lorentzian de Sitter space, where the vertical axis is time and the expanding circles are spatial slices.

Example: Light Cones

Question: In our simulation, the light cone angle is visualized. In a 2D spacetime (1 space + 1 time), a light ray follows ds² = 0. Calculate the angle of the light cone in Lorentzian vs. Euclidean space.

Solution: The light cone defines the paths of light rays, which are the boundaries of causality.

  1. Lorentzian Space:
    ds² = -c²dt² + dx² = 0
    c²dt² = dx²
    c dt = ±dx
    dx/dt = ±c. This describes two lines with slopes +c and -c. If we set c=1, the slopes are ±1, which are lines at 45 degrees from the time axis. This is the familiar light cone. Anything inside can be causally affected.
  2. Euclidean Space:
    ds² = dτ² + dx² = 0
    This equation has only one real solution: dτ = 0 and dx = 0. This means that the only way for the "distance" to be zero is if you don't move at all. There are no light-like paths. The "light cone" has collapsed to a single line (the time axis). Causality is meaningless, as there is no way to connect different points with a signal.
In the simulation, as you move the slider from Lorentzian (α=0) to Euclidean (α=1), you should see the visualized light cones narrow and eventually collapse, demonstrating the dissolution of causal structure.
